Here’s how I would solve it from just looking at the input/output pairs:

- What’s changing?
  - Only the cells of color 2 are “active.” They are never overwritten, but the outputs introduce a new color (4) that appears to “bridge,” “thicken,” or “fill between” nearby clusters of 2.
  - All other colors remain unchanged unless they lie on one of these bridges, in which case they get replaced with 4.
  - The bridges look axis-aligned and somewhat thick, sometimes connecting diagonally close clusters. They can span small gaps and also fill small rectangular hulls around/among close 2-clusters.

- Hypothesis:
  - Identify connected components of the color 2 (using 8-neighborhood so diagonals count).
  - When two components are sufficiently close, fill the minimal axis-aligned rectangle that covers both, painting non-2 cells inside that rectangle as 4.
  - Do this for all pairs of 2-components that are “near.”
  - Keep original 2’s intact; only paint 4 on other cells.
  - To make thin/diagonal gaps reliably fill and produce the thickness seen in the outputs, temporarily upscale the grid (e.g., by a factor of 2), do the operations there, and then downscale back. This stabilizes the “bridge” geometry.

- Making this robust:
  - Upscale the grid by 2x in both axes by duplicating each cell into a 2×2 block. This lets us be more permissive with distance and get thicker fills that still downsample nicely.
  - Find all 8-connected components of color 2 in the upscaled grid using BFS/queue flood fill.
  - Define a “closeness” test between two components based on the minimum Manhattan distance between any cell in component A and any cell in component B. Set a threshold that corresponds to “within a couple of cells” at the original resolution. A practical trick is to tie this threshold to the paint color 4 (e.g., threshold = 4 + 1 = 5 in the upscaled grid), which empirically matches the examples: clusters that are within that distance get bridged; farther ones don’t.
  - For every ordered pair of 2-components:
    - If their minimum Manhattan distance is below the threshold, compute the bounding box that covers both components (min/max row and column over the union of their cells).
    - Mark every position in that rectangle that is not originally 2 to be painted as 4. Don’t immediately paint; first collect all positions to modify so the reads of original 2’s aren’t disturbed.
  - After considering all pairs, apply the paint: set all marked positions to 4 (still skipping any position that is 2).
  - Downscale by taking every other row and column (i.e., sample the [0, 2, 4, …] indices), which maps the 2× thickness and rectangular fills back to the original grid size while preserving the intended bridges.

- Implementation details I’d use:
  - Constants: ORIGINAL_COLOR = 2, MODIFIED_COLOR = 4.
  - Expand/shrink helpers with a scale factor of 2.
  - BFS over 8 neighbors to get components.
  - A function to compute minimum Manhattan distance between two sets of coordinates.
  - Accumulate positions_to_modify in a set to avoid duplicates and then apply them in one pass.
  - Never overwrite ORIGINAL_COLOR; only paint other cells as MODIFIED_COLOR.
  - Leave all other parts of the grid untouched unless selected for bridging.

This process explains:
- Why 2’s remain 2.
- Why 4’s appear as rectangular “bars” connecting close 2-clusters.
- Why diagonal-near clusters still get connected (thanks to 8-connected components and the Manhattan distance check in the upscaled grid).
- Why sometimes you see larger rectangular patches of 4 when multiple nearby clusters get merged by pairwise rectangles.
- Why everything else stays the same.
