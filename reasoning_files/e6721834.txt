Here’s how I would solve it from just looking at the IO pairs, and why the approach matches all three examples.

1) High-level pattern I observe in the IOs
- Each input shows two “big fields” of different background colors (for example 8 vs 0 in the first, 6 vs 1 in the second, 4 vs 8 in the third).
- Inside each big field there are smaller non-background colored pieces (shapes).
- The output is essentially a cropped canvas of one of the two fields’ bounding boxes, filled with that field’s background color, with some non-background shapes placed inside it.
- The relative placement of those shapes in the output aligns with sparse colored “beacons” that already exist in the target field (the recipient) before we paint. These beacons are non-background pixels in the recipient region. The transferred shapes can be rotated or flipped to match.
- Which field is the donor and which is the recipient isn’t always obvious; the correct direction is the one that yields a coherent match to the recipient’s beacons and produces a richer result.

So the task becomes: identify the two big background regions, extract shapes from one, and place them into the other guided by color-matched anchor pixels in the recipient area, allowing rotations/reflections. Try both directions and pick the better.

2) Identify the two background colors and their main regions
- Compute the two most frequent colors in the grid. These are overwhelmingly the backgrounds in all three inputs.
- For each of these two colors, find the largest 4-connected component of that color. That filters out small stray background pixels and isolates the main field of each background color.
- For each of these two main components, compute its bounding box. These two boxes are the donor and recipient canvases, depending on assignment.

3) Set up a directional attempt: donor -> recipient
Given a donor background and a recipient background:
- Donor side: inside the donor bounding box, collect all 4-connected components of non-donor-background cells. These are the “shapes” to transfer. Each shape is multi-colored or single-colored.
- Recipient side: inside the recipient bounding box, treat every pixel that is not the recipient background as an anchor (a beacon) and group anchors by their color. These constraints are what the transferred shapes must align to.

Initialize the output canvas as the recipient bounding box, filled with the recipient background color. Pre-seed it by placing the recipient’s anchor pixels exactly as they are (they act as already-placed colored cells we should not overwrite with a different color).

4) How to place a donor shape in the recipient
- Work in the donor component’s local coordinates (translate it so its min row and min col are at the origin).
- Generate all eight rigid symmetries (D4): the four rotations and their mirror images. This covers rotation/reflection allowed by the IOs.
- For a given transform of the shape, identify which of its colors also exist among the recipient anchors. If none of its colors appear among anchors, skip this transform (no constraints to align on).
- For each color that appears in both the shape and the recipient anchors, compute all translation offsets that would align a shape cell of that color to each anchor of that same color. Take the intersection across all the involved colors so that the placement simultaneously satisfies all colors’ anchor constraints present in the shape. This intersection gives candidate placements.
- For each candidate placement:
  - Check the transformed, translated shape fully fits inside the recipient bounding box.
  - Score the placement by counting how many cells would either paint recipient background or agree with the existing color in the output (including matching anchors). This rewards placements that don’t create conflicts.
  - Track which anchors would be “consumed” (matched) by this placement; later we will avoid reusing the same anchor for multiple shapes.
- Keep all valid candidate placements for that shape, sorted by a desirability score (more matches, more anchor usage, larger coverage).

5) Greedy selection and painting
- Sort donor shapes by priority: first those that share more colors with recipient anchors (i.e., more constrained, more informative), then by larger size.
- For each shape in that order, pick the best placement that doesn’t reuse an anchor already consumed by a previous shape. Paint it onto the output, but only write a color if the current output cell is the recipient background or already that same color (so we never overwrite an existing different-colored anchor).
- Keep a running tally of the total score (how many good paints were made). This measures how well this donor->recipient direction works.

6) Try both directions and choose the better one
- Repeat steps 3–5 with the roles of the two backgrounds swapped (the other field is now donor, and the first is the recipient).
- Compare the aggregate scores of the two attempts and return the result with the higher score.

7) Why this matches the examples
- Example 1: The two big backgrounds are 8 (top field) and 0 (bottom field). The donor shapes inside the 8-field (patterns of 1/2/3) are transplanted into the 0-field region, constrained by sparse 2s and 3s sitting in the 0-field. The shapes are rotated/flipped as needed to satisfy those anchor colors, and the output is the recipient box filled with 0 plus the placed shapes. This exactly explains the two separated blocks of numbers in the output.
- Example 2: The left half (6) contains 3/8 patterns; the right half (1) contains mostly 1 with a few beacons (2, 8). The output is the right half’s bounding box (width 10), filled with 1, embedding the 3/8 patterns aligned to the existing 8 and 2 anchors. The transferred shapes respect the beacons and keep everything else as 1.
- Example 3: The two main regions are 4 and 8. The result is the bounding box of the chosen recipient region filled with that background, plus donor shapes placed in via anchors (notably 1s and 6 act as beacons), with orientation allowed to match.

8) Implementation details I’d use
- 4-neighborhood for connectivity.
- Flood fill (BFS/DFS) both for background main components and for extracting donor shapes within the donor bounding box.
- Bounding boxes computed by min/max rows/cols of component coordinates.
- Recipient anchors stored by color for quick matching.
- For transforms, work in shape-local coordinates and compute the eight symmetries; for each, compute candidate base offsets from anchor matches via set intersections.
- A greedy packing of shapes, avoiding anchor reuse, choosing the best-scoring non-conflicting placement for each.
- Finally, choose the better of the two donor/recipient assignments by comparing scores.

This pipeline explains the cropping sizes, preserved anchors, the appearance of rotations/reflections, and the selective painting behavior visible in all three IO pairs.
