Here’s how I would solve it, based only on the given input/output pairs and what they suggest.

1) Interpret the grid
- The grid is 9x9 with values in {0, 1, 8}.
- 0 clearly acts as background; 1 and 8 are “foreground” and belong to blobs.
- The output is a smaller rectangular subgrid that contains one of the blobs, preserving both 1s and 8s.

2) What blob to pick?
- Each input appears to have multiple foreground blobs separated by 0s.
- The chosen output blob isn’t necessarily the largest by total cell count; it’s the one that maximizes the count of cells equal to 1. The 8s are part of the blob but don’t contribute to this “largest” metric.
- If two blobs tie on the number of 1s, pick the one discovered first during a top-left to bottom-right scan (that’s the natural tie-break you get if you track components in scan order).

3) Connectivity model
- Foreground cells are connected with 4-neighborhood adjacency (up, down, left, right). No diagonals, judging by the shapes and the way they’re cropped in the outputs.

4) Algorithm outline
- Scan the grid row by row, column by column.
- Maintain a visited boolean grid to avoid recounting cells.
- When you find an unvisited, non-zero cell, flood it (BFS or DFS) to collect its entire connected component:
  - Use a queue (BFS is convenient) seeded with the start cell.
  - Only traverse to in-bounds neighbors that are non-zero and not yet visited.
  - While traversing, record for this component:
    - The coordinates of every cell.
    - The value at each cell (so we can count how many 1s are in the component).
- Store each component as a list of (value, (row, col)) pairs or equivalently keep both the value and coordinates for later steps.

5) Choose the target component
- After enumerating all components, select the one with the maximum number of cells whose value is 1.
- In case of ties, the first encountered component (by the outer scan order) will be chosen naturally.

6) Crop to the minimal bounding rectangle of that component
- From the chosen component’s coordinates, compute:
  - min_row, max_row
  - min_col, max_col
- Slice the original grid rows from min_row to max_row, and within each row slice columns from min_col to max_col.
- Return that subgrid as the result, preserving original values and layout.

7) Complexity and notes
- Time: O(R*C) to traverse and label components (R = C = 9 here).
- Space: O(R*C) for visited plus storage of components.
- Assumes there is at least one non-zero cell; otherwise you’d need a defined behavior for “no components.”

This exactly explains why the outputs are the minimal rectangles around the chosen blob and why the selection prefers components rich in 1s even when they include 8s.
