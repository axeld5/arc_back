Here’s how I would solve it, based only on the input/output behavior I see:

1) Interpret the task
- The grid is mostly 0s with a few 2s and one or more blobs/lines of 8s.
- The output draws straight connectors made of 2s from each 2-blob to a nearby 8-blob, choosing either a vertical or a horizontal straight segment, only through zeros.
- At the exact contact point on the 8 side, a 3x3 patch of 8s appears (i.e., the 8 blob gets thickened locally). The exact contact cell itself becomes 2 (so the connector “touches” the 8 mass but occupies the center of the contact).
- If there are multiple 2 blobs and multiple 8 blobs, each 2 blob is connected to a chosen 8 blob, apparently the “closest” along an unobstructed straight row/column. When both vertical and horizontal options exist, it chooses the shorter gap; ties favor vertical.

2) Preprocess: find connected components
- Use 4-connected BFS to find connected components of non-zero values, but keep them grouped by value (value 2 vs value 8). For each component, store:
  - All its cells.
  - Its bounding box and integer center (row, col) as the midpoint of min/max rows/cols.
  - Four edge maps:
    - Top edge: for each column the smallest row in the component at that column.
    - Bottom edge: for each column the largest row at that column.
    - Left edge: for each row the smallest column at that row.
    - Right edge: for each row the largest column at that row.
- These edge maps let us ask “what is the boundary position of this shape along a given column/row?”

3) For every 2-component, consider all 8-components and compute the best straight connection
- We try vertical and horizontal straight connections separately and pick the better option.
- Vertical test:
  - Decide relative vertical orientation from centers: if the 2 center is above the 8 center, we will try to connect the bottom edge of the 2 to the top edge of the 8 (else top of 2 to bottom of 8).
  - Consider only columns that both components occupy (i.e., their edge maps share the column).
  - For each shared column, get the two boundary rows (pos_2 and pos_8). Check that there is at least one cell between them and that every cell strictly between is zero in the original grid (no diagonals, just the column).
  - If clear, compute the gap length = number of zero cells between the two boundaries. Keep the column with the minimal gap.
- Horizontal test:
  - Decide relative horizontal orientation from centers: if the 2 center is left of the 8 center, we try to connect the right edge of the 2 to the left edge of the 8 (else left of 2 to right of 8).
  - Consider only rows that both components occupy.
  - For each shared row, get the two boundary columns (pos_2 and pos_8), ensure at least one cell gap and that all cells between are zero, compute the gap length, and keep the minimal one.
- Choose the direction:
  - If both directions are possible, pick the smaller gap; tie-break in favor of vertical.
  - If neither direction is possible, skip this pair.

4) Record what to draw without altering the original grid while deciding
- For the chosen direction, compute:
  - The straight segment of zeros that will be filled with 2s (connection path).
  - The exact boundary cell on the 8 side that acts as the contact point (the “target”).
- Collect all connection cells (as a set) and all target cells (as a list). Using the set avoids duplicates when paths overlap.

5) Render the result
- Start from a copy of the original grid.
- Paint all connection cells as 2.
- For each target cell on the 8 side:
  - Paint its 3x3 neighborhood (clipped to grid bounds) as 8 to fatten the 8 blob around the contact.
  - Then set the target cell itself to 2 so the connector penetrates one cell into the 8 mass.
- Doing all checks for clear paths against the original grid, and only writing at the end, prevents one newly drawn connector from enabling or blocking another decision.

6) Why this matches the examples
- In the samples, connectors are straight lines of 2s aligned to shared rows or columns where the in-between cells were originally zero.
- The chosen direction is the one with the smallest number of zeros to fill, with vertical favored on ties.
- Around each contact on the 8 side, a 3x3 patch of 8s appears, with the center cell overwritten to 2 as the actual junction.
- Multiple 2- to 8-component pairs are handled independently and can yield multiple connectors.

7) Practical details and edge cases
- Component centers use the bounding box midpoint (integer division).
- Edge maps are computed from the component cells directly.
- Clear-path checks ensure at least one cell gap (no immediate touch through adjacency counts as a connection; we only draw if there’s space to fill).
- Bounds checks when expanding the 3x3 patch.
- Complexity is linear in grid size for BFS plus reasonable additional work for component pair checks.

This reasoning leads directly to an implementation that:
- Extracts 2- and 8-components with BFS.
- Builds per-component edge maps and centers.
- For each 2/8 pair, finds the minimal-gap vertical or horizontal clear corridor.
- Draws the corridor with 2s and thickens the 8 at the contact with a 3x3 neighborhood, then reasserts the contact cell as 2.
