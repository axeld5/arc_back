Here’s how I’d approach this from the given input/output pairs and what that implies about the intended transformation.

High-level hypothesis
- There is a small “key” patch embedded in the grid that defines a 3×3 motif using a few colors plus the background.
- Elsewhere in the grid there are solid, monochrome, axis-aligned square blocks (not background) that act as anchors. Pairs of these squares, when found at matching diagonal positions relative to a 3×3 macro-cell lattice, tell us where to stamp a scaled version of the 3×3 motif.
- The motif may be rotated or flipped; only the two opposite corners of the 3×3 macro-region are initially colored by the input. We must choose the orientation of the motif whose diagonal corner colors match those two anchor squares, then fill the entire 3×3 region at the correct scale, leaving background cells untouched.

Concretely, the steps that follow from the I/O:

1) Identify background color
- The background is the most prevalent color in the grid. In all examples, the dominant color is the blank canvas upon which small colored structures are placed.
- Count all cell colors and take the most frequent one as background b.

2) Locate the embedded 3×3 “key” motif
- Scan the grid for a 3×3 subgrid that contains exactly four distinct colors, one of which is the background.
- That subgrid is the template motif. It is the smallest instance of the pattern to be replicated; it encodes which colors go in each of the 3×3 positions.
- Record its coordinates (top, left), and extract the 3×3 matrix m.
- Keep these coordinates as a “forbidden area” so we don’t double-detect components from the motif itself.

3) Generate all orientations of the motif
- The motif may be used in any rotation and with horizontal/vertical flips. From the examples, the relative arrangement of colors must match anchor colors regardless of orientation.
- Build the set of unique 3×3 variants by:
  - Rotations by 0°, 90°, 180°, 270°
  - For each rotation, also consider the horizontal flip, vertical flip, and vertical flip of the horizontal flip
- Deduplicate identical variants.

4) Find candidate anchor squares elsewhere in the grid
- The anchors are solid, same-color squares of size s×s (s ≥ 1), not background. They are contiguous and axis-aligned.
- To find them robustly, run a flood fill (BFS/DFS) over the grid to find connected components by color (4-connectivity), skipping:
  - background cells
  - cells inside the 3×3 key region
- For each component, get its bounding box. If the bounding box is h×w with h == w, and every cell in that box equals the component color, it’s a perfect solid square. Record:
  - its top-left position (r, c)
  - its size s = h
  - its color col

5) Index squares by position, and prepare to pair them
- Map each detected square by its top-left coordinate for O(1) access.
- We will look for pairs of equal-sized squares that sit at diagonally opposite corners of a 3×3 macro-cell region of size 3s×3s. That means the top-left corners of the two squares differ by exactly ±2s in both row and column:
  - The vector between the two squares is one of: (−2s, −2s), (−2s, +2s), (+2s, −2s), (+2s, +2s).
- Intuition: if you place a 3×3 grid of blocks, each of size s×s, then the 3×3 corners are separated by exactly two block widths in both axes.

6) For each pair, determine the macro 3×3 region and match to a motif orientation
- Given two squares of the same size s at diagonally opposite corners, compute the top-left of the encompassing 3×3 region:
  - It is the min row and min col among the two squares’ top-lefts.
- Label the four macro-corners of the 3×3 region:
  - TL = (top, left)
  - TR = (top, left + 2s)
  - BL = (top + 2s, left)
  - BR = (top + 2s, left + 2s)
- Map the observed pair’s positions to corner labels, and associate each with its color. This gives two constraints: “corner X must have color A” and “corner Y must have color B”.

7) Choose the correct orientation of the motif
- For each precomputed orientation of the 3×3 motif, inspect its diagonal entries:
  - TL = motif[0][0], TR = motif[0][2], BL = motif[2][0], BR = motif[2][2]
- Find the first orientation whose specified corner colors match the two known anchor colors at the appropriate corners.
- This disambiguates rotation/flip automatically.

8) Stamp the scaled motif into the 3×3 region
- We now have:
  - The target 3×3 macro-region top-left (top, left)
  - The block size s
  - The chosen 3×3 motif orientation (call it T)
- For each cell (i, j) in T:
  - If T[i][j] equals the background color, skip (don’t overwrite background).
  - Otherwise, fill the s×s block at (top + i*s, left + j*s) with that color.
- This reproduces the small 3×3 motif at scale s, anchored by the two given diagonally opposite squares.

9) Avoid duplicate application
- Once a pair of squares has been used to stamp a motif, mark both as used to prevent reusing them in another pairing pass.

Why this matches the examples
- In each example, the dominant color is the background.
- There is a single embedded 3×3 patch of four colors including background that acts as the key.
- The rest of the grid contains one or more same-color solid squares that appear at positions consistent with corners of scaled 3×3 macro-regions.
- The outputs show entire 3×3 regions being filled at the scale implied by the square sizes, in an orientation consistent with the anchor colors at the two known corners.
- Background cells in the motif remain background when stamping, which is why the filled areas have “holes” or retain background where the prototype had background.

Edge-handling and robustness decisions
- Only the first 3×3 subgrid matching “four distinct colors including background” is used as the key.
- Components inside the key region are ignored to avoid re-detecting the motif’s cells as anchors.
- Only perfect solid square components, verified by bounding box and uniform color, are treated as anchors.
- Orientation selection is entirely color-constraint driven: the anchor colors must match the corresponding motif diagonal cells for the chosen orientation.
- If no orientation matches, skip that pair; otherwise, paste and continue.

This reasoning leads directly to an implementation that:
- Finds the background and the key 3×3 motif,
- Enumerates all unique rotated/flipped motif variants,
- Detects solid square anchors via BFS,
- Pairs anchors at diagonal offsets consistent with a 3×3 macro-grid at the anchor’s scale,
- Matches an orientation by corner-color constraints,
- Pastes the scaled motif, skipping background cells,
- Marks anchors as used to avoid duplication.
